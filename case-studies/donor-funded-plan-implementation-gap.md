**Composite.** Cedar Landing is a fictional city; this case follows
`downtown-vacancy-carrots-and-sticks.md` by about a year. The pattern — an
engagement-heavy planning process, substantial local donations, a handsome adopted
document, and a first year in which little visible happens — is drawn from the common
arc of small-city downtown planning efforts. No real city, plan, donor, or official is
depicted.


---

## Setting

Cedar Landing, population 5,100. Fourteen months have passed.

## What happened

The city partnered with a university outreach program to run a downtown strategic
visioning process at low direct cost. The process ran in three steps: public input
(one-on-one interviews, four focus groups, a community survey with 380 responses, one
town hall), design (renderings for eleven specific downtown properties and two street
sections), and an action plan (41 numbered action items, each with a named lead, named
partners, step-by-step instructions, and a short-, medium-, or long-term timeline).

Sixty-two individuals and small businesses donated a combined $38,000 to support the
effort. The Main Street manager drove it. Turnout at the town hall was the largest for
any city event in a decade.

The commission adopted the plan unanimously. The document is beautiful. There are 400
printed copies in a closet at city hall.

## Fourteen months later

Of the 41 action items:

- 6 are complete. All six are things the city could do alone with existing staff: a
  downtown cleanup day, replanting eleven tree wells, repairing four streetlights, an
  incentives booklet, a "success stories" page on the city website, and a block-by-block
  audit of public property.
- 4 are in progress.
- 31 have not started. Of those, 22 are assigned to a lead who is not a city employee —
  the DDA chair, the chamber director, individual property owners.
- The two most-requested items in the public survey (angled parking on Water Street, a
  restaurant open past 5:00) are both in the "not started" pile.

The Main Street manager who drove the process has taken a job in another city.

At the last commission meeting, a resident who donated $2,500 asked, publicly, what her
money bought.

## Decision point

You have fifteen minutes on the next agenda. What do you say, and what do you propose?

## What competent practice looks like here

**Do not defend the six.** Leading with the completed items reads as deflection, because
the completed items are the ones that required nothing from anyone. Name the real
number — 6 of 41 — before anyone else does. The manager who reports the unflattering
metric first gets to frame it; the manager who reports it second is being audited.

**The structural problem is assignment, not effort.** Twenty-two action items assigned
to people who do not report to the city was a design flaw in the plan, not a failure of
those people. Volunteers and partner organizations do not have a work plan, a
performance review, or a deadline. Any item whose lead is outside the organization needs
a city-side owner responsible for *chasing* it, or it needs to be reclassified as an
aspiration rather than an action.

**Single-champion dependency is a foreseeable risk.** The plan ran on one person's
energy and she left. This is the ordinary case, not bad luck. Institutionalizing — a
standing quarterly review on the commission agenda, a named staff owner per item, a
public tracker — converts personal momentum into organizational routine. It should have
been built at adoption.

**Donated money creates a reporting obligation that is stronger than the legal one.**
The $38,000 bought the plan, and the plan was delivered. Legally that is the end of it.
Practically, sixty-two donors are now the most motivated constituency in town, and a
public accounting of status — delivered before being demanded — is worth more than the
money. Treating "we already gave you the document" as a sufficient answer converts your
best supporters into your loudest critics.

**Sequencing the restart.** The instinct is to attack the two most-requested items.
Both are long, expensive, and partly outside city control. The better move is to pick
three medium-term items that are visible from the sidewalk, fund them in the coming
budget, and put dates on them — then report the two headline items honestly as
multi-year efforts with a first milestone attached. Visible progress buys the patience
that the big items require.

**Measure something.** The plan has no indicators. Storefront occupancy, downtown
business licenses, and façade grant applications are all countable annually with
existing staff, and give the next conversation a baseline instead of an argument.
