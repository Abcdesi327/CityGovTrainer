## Setting

Cedar Landing, population 5,100, county seat, founded as a river port in the 1830s.
Two- and three-story masonry commercial buildings line four blocks of Water Street. The
county courthouse anchors the north end. A regional manufacturer employs 340 people at
a plant outside town; the plant is stable but not growing.

You are eleven months into your first city manager position. The city commission has
five members and meets twice monthly. Your predecessor held the job for nineteen years.

## Background

Downtown has 118 parcels. A survey done last year for a state redevelopment-zone
application counted 36 with vacant structures — a 30% building vacancy rate. Counting
vacant lots and surface parking, roughly 45% of downtown land is vacant or
underutilized. Fourteen parcels were flagged as blighted.

The city already has more tools than most people realize:

- A façade grant program, roughly $25,000 awarded per year, funded from the general fund
- Low-interest rehabilitation loans through the downtown development authority (DDA)
- A state redevelopment-zone designation approved eighteen months ago, carrying job,
  investment, and rehabilitation tax credits for downtown property owners
- A code enforcement officer, shared half-time with the building department

In your first ten months you have learned three things. Most downtown property owners
cannot name more than one of the four items above. The code enforcement officer has
issued four citations in two years, all for overgrown lots, none downtown. And the
city's own public works annex — a block off Water Street, visible from the courthouse
steps — has peeling paint, a broken security light, and a brick sidewalk that has been
heaving for six years.

## The situation

At a work session, two commissioners press you to "get serious about blight." One has
been reading about vacant property registration ordinances that charge an annual fee
per vacant building, escalating each year the building stays empty. Another has
forwarded you a neighboring city's blight tax, which assesses blighted property at a
multiple of the regular millage rate.

Both mechanisms are real, legally available, and have worked elsewhere. Both would also
land first and hardest on a specific set of about a dozen downtown owners — several of
whom are elderly, several of whom are locally prominent, and at least two of whom
genuinely cannot afford a roof.

A third commissioner, who owns a downtown building, has said nothing during the
discussion.

The mayor asks you to come back in sixty days with a recommendation.

## Decision point

What do you recommend, and in what order?

The naive answer is "both carrots and sticks," which is correct and useless. The real
questions are sequencing, credibility, and who absorbs the first blow.

## What competent practice looks like here

**Sequence matters more than content.** Enforcement mechanisms adopted before owners
know what help exists read as revenue extraction. Incentives offered before enforcement
exists get taken by the owners already inclined to invest and ignored by the ones
causing the problem. The defensible sequence starts with making existing tools known,
because it is cheap, fast, and removes the "nobody told me" defense that will otherwise
be raised at every hearing for the next three years.

**The city goes first.** Adopting minimum maintenance standards for private property
while the city's own annex sits in visible disrepair is the single most predictable way
to lose a code enforcement fight. A block-by-block audit of publicly owned property —
lights, sidewalks, trees, paint — and a visible sprint to fix it costs little and buys
the standing to ask the same of others.

**Enforcement is a political-will problem, not a legal one.** The ordinance is the easy
part. Whether the officer still has a job after the third citation to a well-connected
owner is the actual variable. A manager who secures the ordinance without securing an
explicit commission commitment to back enforcement has set up the officer to fail.

**The silent commissioner is a conflict question, not a personality question.** A
commissioner who owns downtown property has a real financial interest in the outcome of
a blight tax. Whether that requires recusal depends on the jurisdiction's ethics rules
and on whether the effect is particularized or general. The manager's job is to raise it
with the city attorney before the vote, not after.

**Hardship is foreseeable and should be designed for.** A maintenance standard with no
grant pathway attached will produce a demolition, a newspaper story, and a reversal.
Pairing a stabilization or roof grant with the enforcement mechanism is not softness; it
is what makes the mechanism survive contact with its first sympathetic defendant.
